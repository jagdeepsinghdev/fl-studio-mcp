# From a voice-memo idea to a finished record

How a sketch (a hummed melody, a sad piano idea) grows into an arranged hit, and the small
production decisions that make it sound like a record. Distilled from Charlie Puth's breakdown of
"Attention" (Rolling Stone, youtu.be/IU8BEMi8UyM; transcribed locally with Whisper). His statements
are **heard (artist)**; the "use it" lines are **convention**, not auditioned here. Layer roles are in
`melody-layering.md`; forms in `form-and-arrangement.md`; what hits share in `hit-song-patterns.md`.

## 1. Capture by voice, not notation
- He records everything on the road on his phone: melodies sung, shakers, piano. Being "comfortable
  yet uncomfortable" (a hotel room, travelling) is where ideas come.
- Singing the idea is faster than working out its notes ("so much easier than writing E flat").
  Hear the whole thing in your head, then **dissect it into layers** afterwards.
- The phone recordings themselves go straight into the session as parts.
- **Use it here:** hum or sing into the phone, then turn it into notes. The analysis pipeline's
  basic-pitch transcription works on a hummed recording (`learning-from-references.md` commands;
  `fl-melody` writes the notes). Keep the voice memo itself as a texture layer (§5).

## 2. Write it sad, then flip it
- "Attention" began as a **sad, depressing piano ballad**, the way most of his songs start, and then
  became a groovy, fun pop song.
- **Use it:** write the core (chords + melody) slow and bare on a piano, where every note is exposed.
  If it moves you there, keep the notes and change the clothes: tempo up, a syncopated bass, drums,
  brighter instruments (`chord-emotion.md` "instrument colour"). The emotion survives the flip.

## 3. Pick sounds that tell the right story
- His first counter-line synth sounded "sneaky... like a Disney movie", so he replaced it with guitar.
  When a part sounds wrong, **swap the instrument before rewriting the notes**.
- **The fake guitar:** he played the notes one at a time on the phone, cut every waveform, played the
  two notes (E flat and B flat: root and fifth) **twice**, and **panned the two takes apart**: a
  stereo image that "sounds like I know how to play guitar". Double-track + pan = width from one idea.
- **Keep the verse bare:** the Rhodes part "was too vibey to be the verse", so the verse is just the
  phone melody. Save the lush layer for the chorus (`melody-layering.md` §7, hook = add layers).
- **Buried acoustic guitar:** a featured acoustic guitar makes the song "that kind of record"; mixed low
  and used **rhythmically**, it adds motion without changing the genre.

## 4. Model an era, then commit to it
- The album is sonically modelled on **Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, 1988 to early-1990s R&B**: "warm yet
  bright".
- Tools for that: a **fake tape machine** on everything, a little **vinyl crackle** in the background
  (A/B it: the track feels flat without it), and a deliberate **hum** on the bass.
- **Disco bass:** he loved how *bad* disco sounded and took its cool elements. The bass was played,
  **printed** (rendered to audio), then given the same lo-fi treatment.
- **Use it:** name one producer/era as the reference (no more than three, `hit-song-patterns.md` §3),
  list its sound markers, and apply them to every layer so the record feels like one place.
- A **visual scene** can be the brief: his was a hotel room in Miami in 1988, looking at the ocean,
  white linen curtains in the wind. Check every sound against the picture.

## 5. Energy: the anti-drop
- Classic **sweeps** build energy and everyone expects "the drop". He gives them **the anti-drop**:
  the build lands on something smaller or groovier instead of a big hit.
- This is the predictable-plus-surprise rule (`hit-song-patterns.md` §4) applied to a transition:
  set up the expectation fully, then deny it on purpose. Transitions list: `form-and-arrangement.md`.

## 6. Make every chorus a little different
- "You need to make each chorus sound a bit different. It doesn't matter how much you put in, as long
  as it's a little bit different." In one chorus the only change was **a cowbell, pitched up**.
- **Vocal stacking:** the chorus line was sung once, then stacked again and again. "It doesn't sound like
  anything's changing, but it's becoming thicker." Each repeat of the hook can add one more stack.
- **Use it:** list the choruses and give each one new thing (a percussion sound, one more vocal or
  synth double, an octave, a drop-out), never a new melody.

## 7. Real life is the hook
- He was nervous because it sounded like nothing on the radio and nothing like him; it worked because
  it came from real things in his life. People gravitate to that.

## 8. Per-sound craft (from his studio deep dive)
A second session (youtu.be/5N3UUO5nev0) shows the same attitude one sound at a time: play a melody one
note per take and clean every note; **make** a sound (synth + his own voice, formant-shifted) instead of
finding a preset; widen parts with a short delay as they are made, so the centre stays free; give every
sound some movement; nudge hits slightly off the grid. FL steps and stock-plugin stand-ins:
`fl-plugins/references/sound-craft.md`. A third video (youtu.be/c6qAPklUL3E, tips credited to
Babyface, Ross Golan, Julian Bunetta, Skrillex) adds the background-vocal stack, diction, an unexpected cut-off
ending (`fl-mixing/references/background-vocals.md`) and varispeeding the final bounce between keys
(`fl-mixing/references/mastering-stock.md` §2b).

## Checklist
1. Did the idea start as a sung/hummed sketch, and is the core strong played bare on a piano?
2. Does any sound tell the wrong story (too cartoonish, too vibey for the verse)? Swap the instrument first.
3. Is there width from doubling (two takes panned), not only from reverb?
4. One named era/producer, and its markers (tape, crackle, hum) on every layer?
5. Does a build ever deny the expected drop?
6. What is new in each chorus (one sound, one more stack)?
